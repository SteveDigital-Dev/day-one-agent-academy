# Food Chains and Food Webs

## Learning Objectives
- Identify producers, consumers, and decomposers
- Trace energy flow through a food chain
- Explain how food webs show complex relationships in ecosystems

## Producers, Consumers, Decomposers

| Role | Description | Example |
|------|-------------|---------|
| **Producer** | Makes its own food (photosynthesis) | Grass, algae, trees |
| **Consumer** | Eats other organisms for energy | Rabbit, fox, hawk |
| **Decomposer** | Breaks down dead organisms | Fungi, bacteria |

## Types of Consumers

- **Herbivore:** Eats only plants (rabbit, deer, caterpillar)
- **Carnivore:** Eats only meat (fox, hawk, shark)
- **Omnivore:** Eats both plants and meat (bear, human, raccoon)

## Food Chains

A food chain shows energy transfer from one organism to the next.

> Grass → Rabbit → Fox → Hawk

The arrow means "is eaten by" (energy flows in the direction of the arrow).

Each level is called a **trophic level**:
1. Producer (grass)
2. Primary consumer (rabbit)
3. Secondary consumer (fox)
4. Tertiary consumer (hawk)

Energy decreases at each level — only about **10%** of energy passes from one level to the next. That's why food chains are usually only 4–5 levels long.

## Food Webs

Real ecosystems have many interconnected food chains. A **food web** shows all the feeding relationships in an ecosystem.

A food web is more realistic than a single food chain because most organisms eat more than one thing.

## Practice

1. Draw a food chain: grass → grasshopper → frog → snake → hawk. Label each organism as producer, primary consumer, secondary consumer, etc.
2. Why is there less energy available at higher trophic levels?
3. What would happen to the food chain if all the frogs died?
4. Name a decomposer and explain its role.

## Remember

- Producers make food; consumers eat food; decomposers recycle nutrients
- Energy flows from producers up through consumers
- Only about 10% of energy transfers between levels
- Food webs are more realistic than single food chains
