# Persuasive Writing and Argument

## Learning Objectives
- Craft a clear, focused argument (claim)
- Support claims with evidence and reasoning
- Address counterarguments to strengthen your position

## What Makes Persuasive Writing Different?

**Persuasive writing** tries to convince the reader to agree with you or take action. Unlike informative writing, it takes a clear stance.

## Building an Argument

**Claim:** What you want the reader to believe.
> "Schools should start later for teenagers."

**Evidence:** Facts, statistics, expert opinions that support your claim.
> "Studies show that teens who sleep 8+ hours perform 20% better on tests."

**Reasoning:** Explain *why* the evidence supports your claim.
> "Because teens' biological clocks shift later, early start times force them to sleep during their natural wake cycle, reducing alertness."

## Types of Evidence

| Type | Example |
|------|---------|
| **Statistics** | "85% of teens get less than 8 hours of sleep" |
| **Expert quote** | "The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends..." |
| **Example** | "In Seattle, when schools shifted to 8:45..." |
| **Anecdote** | "My friend struggles to stay awake in 7 AM classes" |

## Addressing Counterarguments

Acknowledge the other side, then explain why your position is stronger.

> "Some argue that later start times would disrupt after-school activities. However, Seattle's schedule change showed that activity participation actually increased because students were more awake and engaged."

## Practice

Write a persuasive paragraph on: "Should students have homework on weekends?"

1. State your claim
2. Provide one piece of evidence
3. Explain your reasoning
4. Address one counterargument

## Remember

- A claim without evidence is just an opinion
- The strongest arguments acknowledge the other side
- Reasoning is the bridge between evidence and claim
- Stay respectful — attacks weaken your argument
