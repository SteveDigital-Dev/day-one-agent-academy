# Drawing Inferences

## Learning Objectives
- Define inference as a conclusion based on evidence and reasoning
- Use text clues and background knowledge to make inferences
- Distinguish between what the text says and what the text means

## What Is an Inference?

An **inference** is a conclusion you draw based on what the text says plus what you already know. It's reading "between the lines."

> Text: "Maria put on her raincoat and grabbed an umbrella."
> Inference: **It is raining or about to rain.**

The text never says "it's raining," but the clues let us figure it out.

## The Inference Formula

**Text Evidence + What I Know = Inference**

| Text Says | I Know | Inference |
|-----------|--------|-----------|
| "Tom's stomach growled loudly." | Stomach growling means hunger | Tom is hungry |
| "The playground was empty and wet." | Empty + wet usually means rain | It recently rained |

## Common Inference Mistakes

**Guessing without evidence:** "Maria is going to the beach." (No evidence supports this.)

**Restating the text:** "Maria has an umbrella." (That's what the text says, not an inference.)

A good inference goes **beyond** the text while staying grounded in it.

## Practice

Read each passage and make an inference:

1. "James checked his watch three times during the meeting and kept glancing at the door."
   - What can you infer about James?

2. "The garden was covered in fallen leaves, and the trees were bare."
   - What season is it? What evidence supports your inference?

3. "Lena smiled when she opened the package and hugged her mother."
   - What can you infer about the package?

## Remember

- Inferences are educated guesses, not wild guesses
- Always base inferences on text evidence
- Use your background knowledge to fill in gaps
- Two readers can make different valid inferences from the same text
